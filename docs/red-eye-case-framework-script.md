# Red Eye: Case Framework Script

Oct 30, 2026

## Concept and core loop

Red Eye's existing story already starts in the right place: you fall asleep over page 38 of the casebook the night before a 9 a.m. interview, wake up inside the airline, and have to get one red-eye out by dawn. This script makes every gate in that world a case you frame.

**Structure**

| Area | Host | Cases | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening: Hangar and Ops Tower | Moss | Tutorial, operations and cost (required), M&A (bonus) | Access to the three branches |
| Branch 1: Tank Farm | Okafor | Profitability decline or market sizing | Fuel chit |
| Branch 2: Baggage Hall | Reyes | Market entry or PE due diligence | Loadsheet |
| Branch 3: Terminal | Dana | Pricing or growth strategy | Manifest |
| Finale: Remote Stand | The aircraft | Two boarding doors, then one unseen case | Pushback before dawn |

**Two gates per item.** Each branch holds two cases. The run picks one at random to guard that branch's item, and the other stays open as a bonus case for stars. Replays swap which case guards which item, so every playthrough tests a different mix.

**The seven-beat case loop.** Every gate runs the same loop, mapped to the opening routine in the prep guide.

1. **Hear the Prompt.** The host states the problem in character.
2. **Lock the Objective.** Assemble a one-sentence restatement from tiles: a goal verb, a metric, a timeframe. A missing metric or timeframe means you must ask for it.
3. **Spend Lanterns.** You hold 3 Question Lanterns. Six questions are on offer; three earn useful facts, three are questions the structure would answer anyway.
4. **Build the Board.** Pick 3 or 4 bucket cards (phrased as questions) from a deck of about 8, then hang 2 to 4 sub-point cards under each. Decoys include label-only buckets, overlapping buckets, generic sub-points and off-topic ideas.
5. **Plant the Flag.** Choose which bucket to start in and pick a hypothesis.
6. **Drive the Case.** Two data cards arrive. For each, tag which bucket it updates and choose the "so what."
7. **Close.** Assemble an answer-first recommendation: the call, two reasons with numbers, the top risk, a next step. Then the host asks the Partner's Question, drawn from the bucket you ranked last, so no branch of your board is safe to ignore.

**Design rules**

- Each host is built around one trait from their portrait, and that trait is the trap in their case.
- You are the hint system: your own thoughts escalate from nudge to near-answer.
- No failure states: a wrong move opens a coaching moment, never a game over.
- Everything clickable talks back, and repeat clicks escalate.
- Every case has an aviation version (Option A) and a non-aviation version (Option B), so you practice tailoring to more than one industry.
- The aircraft's existing sorting puzzle (supply, demand, cost, price) can stay as the warm-up before the boarding doors.

## Cast

Your five existing characters host every case, and each one's portrait trait becomes the trap their cases test.

| Character | Where | Portrait | The one trait | The trap it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| You | Everywhere | Silhouette | Thinks out loud | Your thoughts are the hint system |
| Moss | Hangar, Ops Tower | Coveralls, ear defenders, smudged cheek | Up too long, wants to fix the loudest problem first | Biggest isn't the same as addressable; a fixer-upper isn't a deal |
| Okafor | Tank Farm, Fuel Price Shack | High-vis vest, clipboard, dry half-smile | Has heard every question before | Generic questions waste time; it isn't always fuel; give a number, not a lecture |
| Reyes | Baggage Hall, Ramp Office | Headset, tablet, half-turned away | About to walk off mid-sentence | Answer first; a big market isn't a winnable one; a fund has no synergies |
| Dana | Gate, Lounge | Uniform, neck scarf, hair coming loose | Exhausted but kind, wants every passenger happy | A discount for everyone isn't pricing; yes to every idea isn't strategy |
| The aircraft | Remote Stand | Jet nose, cockpit-window eyes | Calm, patient, asks the last question | Plays the Partner: the finale and every Partner's Question |

**Reyes's patience meter.** In both of his cases a small meter drains while you hesitate. It never fails you, but every close that leads with the answer refills it, and he turns back to face you. It's the cheapest way to drill answer-first communication.

**Okafor's lantern rule.** In his cases a wasted question costs two lanterns instead of one, because he has heard it before. His dry half-smile frame plays every time.

**Clickpoint regulars** (no case, just reactions):

- **Cold coffee** on your desk, which rounds every number it hears.
- **The PA speaker**, which answers every question with "It depends."
- **The departure board**, which keeps flipping to new delay reasons.
- **Page 38**, which follows you around the dream and reads out one airline term each time you click it.

## Scoring, coaching and randomization

Each case scores the five things every firm tests, and a wrong move never ends the case: it opens a coaching moment that can still earn points.

**The five gauges** (0 to 5 stars each per case):

| Gauge | What earns it | Where it is scored |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | 3 or 4 buckets, phrased as questions, no overlap, nothing critical missing | Beats 2 and 4 |
| Analytics | Correct math, insight stated before numbers | Beat 6 |
| Judgment | Industry-specific sub-points, sensible hypothesis, right starting bucket | Beats 4 and 5 |
| Communication | Answer-first close, "so what" after every data card | Beats 6 and 7 |
| Presence | Fixing a mistake after one hint, holding a correct position under pushback | Any coaching moment |

**Board scoring rules** for Beat 4:

- +1 Structure per critical bucket placed (each case has 3 or 4 critical buckets).
- -1 Structure for a fifth bucket, an overlapping bucket, or a label-only bucket ("Market" instead of "Is the market attractive?").
- +1 Judgment per industry-specific sub-point; 0 for generic ones ("fixed and variable costs").
- The Delete Test: after the board is built, the host asks "If we answered every bucket, could we answer the question?" A missing bucket gets a raised eyebrow; a pointless bucket gets a shrug.

**Coaching moments.** When the player picks a decoy, the host reacts in character and you get a chance to revise. Revising right away earns 1 Presence star ("coachable"). Pushback on a correct choice is also possible; holding firm with a reason earns Presence too.

**The hint ladder.** Clicking your thought bubble climbs three levels. Each level used costs half a star in that beat.

1. Nudge: you restate the host's goal to yourself.
2. Pointer: you name which bucket or tile is missing, without the answer.
3. Near-answer: you say what you would write, phrased as a question to yourself.

**Randomization.** Each case has three slots with two options each, giving 8 versions of every case.

| Slot | What it swaps | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry | Which industry the client is in | Forces industry-specific sub-points |
| Twist | What the data reveals underneath | Prevents memorizing the answer |
| Data | Which bucket the first exhibit lands in | Forces driving from the board, not a script |

The Partner's Question at the close is always drawn from the bucket the player ranked last, so every bucket on the board has to hold up.

**Ranks.** Total stars across the 8 cases unlock titles: Analyst (start), Associate (40 stars), Engagement Manager (100), Partner (160, out of 200 possible).

## Opening: Your Desk and Moss's Tutorial

The opening teaches all seven beats on a tiny case in the Hangar, so the player learns the loop before the branches randomize it.

**Scene 1: Your desk, 11 p.m.** *Page 38 open, cold coffee, a sticky note that says "9 A.M." in capitals.*

**YOU** *(thinking)*: Thirty-seven airline terms. Interview at nine. I'll just rest my eyes for one term.

*The desk lamp hums and stretches into a jet bridge. You walk down it, half asleep, and step out onto a dark apron. A red-eye sits at the far stand with its door open and no crew.*

**Scene 2: The Hangar.** *Moss is half inside an engine cowling. Only her boots and a flashlight beam are visible.*

**MOSS:** If you're the new consultant, hand me the 7/16. If you're not, also hand me the 7/16.

**YOU:** I'm... sort of an interviewee.

**MOSS:** *Slides out.* Close enough. That plane at the remote stand needs to leave before dawn, and nobody here will sign off on anything until someone thinks clearly at them. Warm up on me.

**MOSS:** Maintenance cost per flight hour on this engine type is up 20% this quarter. Why?

**Beat 2, Lock the Objective.**

| Tile set | Options | Correct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | find why cost rose and how to bring it down / fix the engine / blame the manufacturer | find why cost rose and how to bring it down |
| Metric | maintenance cost per flight hour / number of wrenches / vibes | maintenance cost per flight hour |
| Timeframe | this quarter vs last / forever / ? (ask) | this quarter vs last |

**YOU:** So you want to know why maintenance cost per flight hour rose 20% versus last quarter, and how to bring it down.

**MOSS:** That's the first useful sentence anyone's said in here since Tuesday.

**Beat 3, Spend Lanterns** (3 lanterns).

| Question | Useful? | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did the cost per repair rise, or the number of repairs? | Yes | "Both moved. Parts prices went up, and we're pulling more engines off wing." |
| Did flight hours change? | Yes | "Down 10%. We parked two planes." |
| What does success look like? | Yes | "Back to last quarter's rate within six months." |
| Is it costs? | No | "It's literally called maintenance cost." |
| Is the engine bad? | No | "Define bad." |
| Can I hold the flashlight? | No | "No." |

**Beat 4, Build the Board.** Teaches the cost-per-unit tree: a ratio rises when the top goes up or the bottom goes down.

| Bucket card | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Did total maintenance spend rise (price per part, number of repairs, labor)? | Critical |
| Did flight hours fall, spreading the same cost thinner? | Critical |
| Is it this fleet or every airline's (are suppliers raising prices for everyone)? | Critical |
| Costs | Decoy, label only. Moss: "That's a word. I need a question." |
| Engines | Decoy. Moss: "I know what's in the hangar, thanks." |

**Beat 5, Plant the Flag.** Best start: flight hours, because Moss already said they fell 10%. Hypothesis: "Half the rise is fewer flight hours spreading fixed maintenance thinner."

**Beat 6, Drive.** Data card: "Total spend up 8%. Flight hours down 10%." So what: "1.08 divided by 0.90 is a 20% rise per hour. Most of it is the denominator, not the wrench work."

**Beat 7, Close.**

**YOU:** Cost per flight hour rose 20% mostly because we flew 10% less while spending 8% more. Get the two parked planes flying, and renegotiate the parts contract. The risk is the parts price keeps climbing, so next step is quoting a second supplier.

**MOSS:** *Wipes her hands.* Fine. You can think. Now do a real one.

*You receive the Question Lanterns, the Objective tiles and the Hypothesis Flag, drawn in grease pencil on the back of a work order.*

## The Hangar with Moss (operations and cost reduction)

Required to leave the opening. The trap is Moss's instinct to fix the loudest problem: the player must find the most addressable cost and pick the right lever (volume, rate, productivity, structure).

**Variants**

| Slot | Option A | Option B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry | Airline maintenance base | Regional parcel distribution center |
| Twist | Parts are the biggest cost but locked by long-term contracts; repeat defects and wrench time are the lever | Labor is the biggest cost and is addressable through scheduling |
| Data | First exhibit is the cost stack | First exhibit is a process step (rework or throughput) |

**Hear the Prompt**

*Moss is back under the cowling. A clipboard on the tool cart reads "CUT 10%" in red marker.*

**MOSS:** Corporate wants 10% out of this base in a year. Parts are half the bill, so we cut parts. Done. Can I go back to my engine?

**YOU:** Can we cut parts?

**MOSS:** *Long pause.* ...Define "cut."

**Lock the Objective.** Correct: "Find 10% of base cost to remove within 12 months without hurting safety or on-time departures." A tile without the safety constraint makes Moss drop her wrench: "The FAA would like a word. Safety stays."

**Spend Lanterns**

| Question | Useful? | Answer (A / B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does the cost stack look like? | Yes | A: parts 50%, labor 30%, overhead 10%, rework and repeat defects 10%. B: labor 55%, transport 25%, facility 20%. |
| What can't change? | Yes | A: parts prices locked under contract through next year; safety procedures fixed. B: delivery promises fixed. |
| What do you measure today? | Yes | A: repeat defect rate, wrench time around 45% of a shift. B: packages per labor hour, overtime hours. |
| Is cost bad? | No | "Generally." |
| Why are you always under an engine? | No | "Because it's quieter than meetings." |
| Can we just park the planes? | No | Moss stares. You go back to the board. |

**Build the Board**

| Bucket card | Status | Correct sub-points (A / B) | Decoy sub-points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where is the cost, and how much is addressable? | Critical | Cost stack by bucket, share locked by contract or regulation | "Costs" (label only) |
| Can we do less of it (volume)? | Critical | A: repeat defects, rework, unscheduled removals. B: re-handling, empty miles | "Fix fewer things" |
| Can we pay less (rate) or do it faster (productivity)? | Critical | A: wrench time, parts staging, overtime. B: shift scheduling, overtime, route density | "Work harder" |
| Should we change the structure? | Critical | Make vs buy (outsource heavy checks), consolidate shifts, tooling and automation payback | "Fire everyone" (Moss drops her wrench) |
| Are parts the biggest cost? | Decoy, already answered | | |
| Are costs high and expensive? | Decoy, overlaps | | |

**Plant the Flag.** Best start: "Where is the cost, and how much is addressable?" Hypothesis A: "Parts are locked, so the 10% comes from repeat defects and wrench time." Hypothesis B: "Overtime from poor scheduling is the biggest addressable bucket."

**Drive the Case**

- Data A1: "Base cost $100M. Parts $50M, locked. Rework and repeat defects $10M; 1 in 8 jobs comes back." So what: "Half the cost can't move this year, so 10% of the total means 20% of what's addressable."
- Data B1: "Overtime is 18% of labor hours; volume peaks are predictable on Mondays." So what: "Overtime is a scheduling problem, so staff up for Mondays."
- Math card A: "Halving repeat defects saves half of $10M. Raising wrench time from 45% to 55% cuts labor per job about 18% on a $30M labor base. Total?" Answer: $5M plus about $5.4M, roughly $10.4M, just over the 10% target.

**Close (model answer, Twist A)**

**YOU:** We can hit 10% without touching parts. Parts are half the cost but locked by contract, so the savings come from halving repeat defects, worth $5M, and raising wrench time from 45% to 55% by staging parts before each shift, worth about $5.4M. The risk is rushing safety checks, so next step is a root-cause review of the top three repeat defects.

**MOSS:** So... not parts.

**YOU:** Not this year.

**MOSS:** *Puts the wrench down.* Huh. Go on, then. The branches are open.

**Partner's Question** (from the lowest-ranked bucket, example): "Did you price outsourcing the heavy checks? That's a structure lever."

**Hint ladder**

1. "Biggest isn't the same as movable."
2. "How much of each bucket can actually change in 12 months?"
3. "Find the addressable base first, then pick levers: volume, rate, productivity, structure."

**Clickpoints**

- Tool cart (1) "Clank." (2) "Clank, clank." (3) "Every clank is a repeat defect, by the way."
- Wrench-time gauge (1) "45%." (2) "Still 45%." (3) "I could be 55% if someone staged the parts. Just saying."

## The Ops Tower with Moss (corporate M&A)

Bonus case in the opening area. The trap is Moss's fixer instinct: a broken company she could fix is not a good deal unless synergies, net of the cost to get them, beat the premium.

**Variants**

| Slot | Option A | Option B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry | Your airline buying a smaller regional carrier | Industrial parts distributor buying a regional competitor |
| Twist | Synergies fall short of the premium, so walk away or bid lower | Synergies cover the premium, but integration is the real risk |
| Data | First exhibit lands in synergies | First exhibit lands in standalone value |

**Hear the Prompt**

*The ops tower at night. The wall map has new pins, and two airline logos have been taped together with duct tape. Moss is up here on a coffee break she isn't taking.*

**MOSS:** Board wants to buy the regional across the field. Their fleet is a mess. I could fix it in a year. Tell me that's a good reason.

**YOU:** What's the price?

**MOSS:** A 40% premium. Which is fine, because I could fix it.

**Lock the Objective.** Correct: "Decide whether buying the regional at a 40% premium creates value for our shareholders." Decoy tile "fix their fleet" gets Moss's grin: "Finally, someone who gets it." Then your thought bubble: "Fixing it is a plan, not a price."

**Spend Lanterns**

| Question | Useful? | Answer (A / B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why this target, strategically? | Yes | A: their gates and slots at two busy airports. B: fills gaps in our regional network. |
| What's the price, and what's it worth on its own? | Yes | $1.4B price; standalone value $1.0B. |
| How would we combine them? | Yes | A: shared maintenance, one booking system, combined schedules. B: shared warehouses, purchasing. |
| Is their fleet a mess? | No | "Extremely. That's not a question, that's a fact." |
| Can you really fix it in a year? | No | "Yes. Next question." |
| Who else is bidding? | No | "Interesting. Your board should answer it." |

**Build the Board**

| Bucket card | Status | Correct sub-points (A / B) | Decoy sub-points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Does the deal make strategic sense? | Critical | Fit with our network, alternatives (grow organically, codeshare) | "Moss could fix it" |
| What is the target worth on its own? | Critical | Standalone growth, margins, fleet age, quality of earnings | "Revenue" (label only) |
| What synergies are real, and what do they cost? | Critical | A: maintenance and overhead cost savings, network revenue. B: warehouse consolidation, purchasing. Timing and cost to achieve | "Synergies" (no detail) |
| Does price beat value, and can we integrate? | Critical | Premium vs synergy value, financing; A: merging pilot seniority lists and booking systems. B: three ERP systems | "Pay whatever" |
| Are their planes old? | Decoy, already answered | | |
| Is the target good and valuable? | Decoy, overlaps standalone value | | |

**Plant the Flag.** Best start: synergies vs premium, because that decides the deal. Hypothesis A: "Synergies won't cover a 40% premium." Hypothesis B: "The math works; integration is the risk."

**Drive the Case**

- Data A1: "Cost synergies $20M a year from year 2; network revenue uncertain. Cost to achieve $50M." So what: "Even valued at 10x, cost synergies fall well short of the $400M premium."
- Data B1: "Synergies $60M a year at a 10x multiple; integrating three ERP systems over 2 years." So what: "Synergies are worth about $600M, so price is fine; the risk is the systems integration."
- Math card: "Price $1.4B, standalone $1.0B. Synergies $20M a year at 10x, minus $50M to achieve. Value created?" Answer: $200M minus $50M is $150M against a $400M premium, so the deal destroys about $250M.

**Close (model answer, Twist A)**

**YOU:** Don't buy at this price. The premium is $400M, but net synergies are worth about $150M, so the deal destroys roughly $250M. If the gates and slots matter most, bid no more than a 15% premium, or buy the slots alone. The risk is a rival taking them, so next step is checking whether the slots can be leased without buying the airline.

**MOSS:** So I don't get to fix their fleet.

**YOU:** Not at a 40% premium.

**MOSS:** *Sighs at the duct-taped logos.* Fair.

**Hint ladder**

1. "A company I could fix and a good deal aren't the same thing."
2. "I need to compare what we pay above its value with what we gain."
3. "Do synergies, net of cost, beat the 40% premium? That decides it."

**Clickpoints**

- Duct-taped logos (1) "We're one company now." (2) "Emotionally." (3) "Legally, check back in 18 months."
- Wall map pins (1) "New route!" (2) "Another new route!" (3) "Has anyone asked if these routes make money?"

## The Tank Farm with Okafor (profitability decline)

One of two possible gates for the fuel chit. The trap is Okafor's certainty that it's always fuel: the player must find where the decline is concentrated before naming a cause, and sometimes Okafor is right.

**Variants**

| Slot | Option A | Option B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry | Regional airline, 40 aircraft | Regional grocery chain, 60 stores |
| Twist | Client problem: a low-cost rival is pulling down fares on our routes | Market problem: an industry-wide cost spike (A: jet fuel, B: wages) |
| Data | First exhibit lands in the revenue bucket | First exhibit lands in the client-vs-market bucket |

**Hear the Prompt**

*Rows of silver tanks under sodium lights. Okafor watches a gauge needle with the patience of a man who has watched it for 30 years.*

**OKAFOR:** Profit's down 15% over two years. Before you ask: yes, it's fuel. It's always fuel.

**YOU:** Is it always fuel?

**OKAFOR:** *Dry half-smile.* That's the first question everyone asks. You've used one lantern.

**Lock the Objective.** Correct: "Find why profit fell 15% over two years and recommend how to recover it within 18 months." Decoy tile "lower the fuel price" gets: "Wonderful. Call OPEC for me."

**Spend Lanterns** (Okafor's rule: a wasted question costs two lanterns)

| Question | Useful? | Answer (A / B) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did revenue fall, or did costs rise, or both? | No | "Heard it. Your board answers that." (costs two) |
| Are competitors seeing the same decline? | Yes | A: "The low-cost carrier across the field just added six routes." B: "Everyone's complaining about payroll." |
| How do you make money, and what changed? | Yes | A: mostly leisure fares, some business, small cargo. B: groceries, private label growing slowly. |
| What does recovery look like, and by when? | Yes | Back to prior margin within 18 months. |
| Is it fuel? | No | "Yes." (costs two) |
| Do you ever leave the tank farm? | No | "Once. Didn't care for it." (costs two) |

**Build the Board**

| Bucket card | Status | Correct sub-points (A / B) | Decoy sub-points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is the decline on the revenue side? | Critical | A: load factor, yield (fare per passenger mile), mix of business vs leisure, route-level revenue. B: traffic vs basket size, private label mix, price gap vs discounters | "Sales" (label only) |
| Is it on the cost side? | Critical | A: fuel, labor, maintenance, airport fees. B: cost of goods, labor hours, shrink, distribution | "Fixed and variable" (generic) |
| Is it the market or the client? | Critical | Competitor margins, share trend on our routes or in our markets | "Fuel, obviously" (Okafor nods) |
| What levers would fix it? | Critical | A: route pruning, fuel hedging, fare fencing, ancillaries. B: private label, promo discipline, store closures | "Buy cheaper fuel" |
| Fuel | Decoy, label only | | |
| Are revenues and sales down? | Decoy, overlaps revenue | | |

**Plant the Flag.** Best start: "Is it the market or the client?" because it decides whether Okafor is right. If Twist B is live and you pick "industry cost spike," Okafor pushes back ("Now you agree with me? Why?") and holding firm with the data earns a Presence star.

| Hypothesis | Twist A | Twist B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A rival is pulling down our fares | Correct | Coaching moment |
| An industry-wide cost spike is squeezing everyone | Coaching moment | Correct |
| It's always fuel | Okafor's half-smile; your thought bubble: "Always is not a hypothesis." | Partial credit until you say why |

**Drive the Case**

- Data A1 (revenue bucket): "Load factor steady at 82%. Yield down 9%, all of it on the six routes the low-cost carrier entered." So what: "Planes are full; fares fell. It's a pricing problem on six routes, not fuel."
- Data B1 (market bucket): "Our margin fell 3 points; rivals' margins fell 3 points too; jet fuel up 30%." So what: "It's industry-wide, so look at the shared cost driver. This time it really is fuel."
- Math card: "Profit was $40M. It's now $34M. What percent drop?" Answer: 15%.

**Close (model answer, Twist A)**

**YOU:** Profit fell because fares dropped 9% on the six routes where a low-cost carrier entered, while load factor held. Respond route by route: match fares where we hold the better slots, and pull capacity where we can't win. The risk is a fare war spreading to other routes, so next step is ranking all six routes by profit after the fare drop.

**OKAFOR:** *Long look at the gauge.* Not fuel.

**YOU:** Not this time.

**OKAFOR:** Hm. *Tears a fuel chit off his pad and hands it over.*

**Hint ladder**

1. "Okafor wants profit back. Profit has two halves."
2. "I haven't checked whether everyone else is hurting too."
3. "Is this the market or just us? That should be a bucket."

**Clickpoints**

- Fuel gauge (1) "Needle at half." (2) "Still half." (3) "Half full or half empty is a framing question. Frame it."
- Okafor's clipboard (1) "Gallons." (2) "More gallons." (3) "A list of every question he's heard. Yours is on page 4."

## The Fuel Price Shack with Okafor (market sizing)

The other possible gate for the fuel chit. The trap is giving Okafor a lecture instead of a number: the player picks top-down or bottom-up, rounds early, states assumptions out loud and sanity-checks.

In this case the Board is an estimation tree instead of buckets: the player picks an approach card, then chains driver cards.

**Variants**

| Slot | Option A | Option B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry | Annual jet fuel sales at a mid-size US airport (bottom-up from departures) | US annual spend on electric toothbrush replacement heads (top-down from households) |
| Twist | One driver card uses the wrong unit (per hour vs per departure) | One driver card is a sneaky double count |
| Data | Sanity check against a large hub's known volume | Sanity check against the category's retail sales |

**Hear the Prompt**

*A tin shack with a price board rewritten so many times the old numbers show through. Okafor leans in the doorway.*

**OKAFOR:** A supplier wants to bid on our fuel contract. How big is the contract, per year, in dollars? I don't want a lecture. I want a number.

**YOU:** How exact?

**OKAFOR:** Exact enough that I don't laugh.

**Lock the Objective.** Correct: "Estimate annual jet fuel sales at this airport, in dollars, within a reasonable range, in under 5 minutes." Tile "to the gallon" gets: "I said a number, not a hobby."

**Spend Lanterns** (wasted questions cost two)

| Question | Useful? | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dollars per year, or gallons? | Yes | Dollars per year. |
| Commercial flights only, or cargo and private too? | Yes | Commercial passenger flights only. |
| Roughly how many departures a day? | Yes | "About 300." |
| Can I see last year's invoices? | No | "If I had those, why would I need you?" (costs two) |
| How much does a plane hold? | No | "Depends on the plane. Estimate." (costs two) |
| What's the answer? | No | *Half-smile.* (costs two) |

**Build the Tree (Option A, bottom-up)**

| Driver card | Status | Value to state out loud |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Departures per day | Correct | About 300 |
| Gallons uplifted per departure (mix of regional and mainline) | Correct | About 2,000 |
| Days per year | Correct | About 365, call it 350 for rounding |
| Price per gallon | Correct | About $3 |
| Gallons burned per flight hour | Decoy, wrong unit. Thought bubble: "Per hour? I counted departures, not hours." | |
| Passengers per day | Decoy, not a fuel driver here | |

**Option B, top-down** chains: about 130M US households, 40% with an electric toothbrush user, 2 users per household, about 3 heads per user a year, about $5 a head. Decoy: "US population times households" (double count).

**Plant the Flag.** Here the flag is the approach choice. Bottom-up fits A because departures are countable; top-down fits B because households are a clean starting number. Picking the other approach earns a coaching moment, not a penalty, if you explain why.

**Drive the Case**

- Math card A: 300 departures times 2,000 gallons is 600,000 gallons a day; times 350 days is about 210M gallons; times $3 is about $630M a year.
- Data A1, sanity check: "The busiest US hubs sell well over 1 billion gallons a year." So what: "A mid-size airport at a fifth of that is plausible."
- Math card B: 130M times 40% is about 50M households; times 2 users is 100M; times 3 heads is 300M heads; times $5 is about $1.5B.

**Close (model answer, Option A)**

## The Gate with Dana (pricing)

One of two possible gates for the manifest. The trap is Dana's kindness: treating every passenger the same is not pricing, so the player must weigh cost floor, competitor reference and customer value, then segment.

**Variants**

| Slot | Option A | Option B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry | Airline raising transcontinental fares 5% | Streaming service raising its monthly plan by $2 |
| Twist | Business demand barely reacts, so a targeted raise works | The main rival won't follow, so a flat raise loses share; tiering wins |

**Hear the Prompt**

*Gate B7, nearly empty. Dana is behind the desk with her scarf loosened and a stack of rebooking slips.*

**DANA:** Revenue management wants transcon fares up 5%. My passengers already look at me like I personally invented bag fees. Can we just... not? Or raise them for nobody?

**YOU:** Raise them for nobody?

**DANA:** *Tired laugh.* It's 2 a.m. Help me think.

**Lock the Objective.** Correct: "Decide whether a 5% fare increase raises profit on these routes over the next year." Decoy tile "keep every passenger happy" gets a soft "I wish" from Dana, then your thought bubble: "Happy isn't a metric. Profit is."

| --- | --- | --- |

| Bucket card | Status | Correct sub-points (A / B) | Decoy sub-points |

**Drive the Case**

- Data A1: "Business demand drops 2% for a 5% increase; leisure drops 10%." So what: "Business travelers barely react, so aim the raise at them."

Leisure demand drops 10%, so hold leisure fares where Dana's passengers are most price-sensitive. **DANA:** So the families on the red-eye don't pay more.

## Finale at the Remote Stand

With the fuel chit, loadsheet and manifest in hand, the player brings them to the aircraft, which tests both interview formats and then asks one case nobody has seen before.

**Scene 1: The Remote Stand.** *The red-eye sits alone under floodlights. Its cockpit windows open slowly, like eyes.*

**AIRCRAFT:** Fuel. Weight. Passengers. You've brought me everything I need to fly.

**YOU:** So we can go?

**AIRCRAFT:** Almost. A plane can carry anything. The hard part is explaining why it should.

*Optional warm-up: the aircraft's existing sorting puzzle (supply, demand, cost, price) plays here if you keep it.*

**Scene 2: The Two Boarding Doors.** *Both of the aircraft's doors open. Stairs roll up to each one.*

| Door | Format | How it plays | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Forward door | Interviewer-led | The aircraft asks five fixed questions in order: structure, a brainstorm, an exhibit, a math problem, a synthesis. You can't steer. | Depth on each piece; structuring a brainstorm before speaking |
| Aft door | Candidate-led | The aircraft gives only the prompt, then waits. You ask for data and drive from your board. | Driving the case, prioritizing, asking for the right data |

Each door draws a random case from the 8 above, in an industry not yet seen (for example, a hospital, a telecom carrier, a utility or a chemical maker), so tailoring is tested on unfamiliar ground.

**AIRCRAFT** *(forward door)*: What factors would you consider?

**YOU:** Two groups, then three ideas in each.

**AIRCRAFT:** *Its nose dips slightly.* Very few people structure a brainstorm.

**AIRCRAFT** *(aft door)*: ...

**YOU:** May I see margins by segment?

**AIRCRAFT:** Better. You asked for what you needed instead of waiting for it.

**Scene 3: The Last Case.** *The floodlights dim. The horizon starts to turn gray.*

**AIRCRAFT:** One more. No cards this time. No deck. Just a question, and the first sentence is yours.

*The final case is a non-standard prompt drawn from a pool (examples: "An airport wants to cut security wait times in half"; "A city's transit ridership fell after fares went to zero"; "An airline must cut emissions 30% by 2030"). No bucket deck is offered. The player first chooses a scaffold card:*

| Scaffold card | Fits prompts like |
| --- | --- |
| Objective, options, criteria | Any decision with several paths |
| Stakeholders or value chain walk | Public sector, healthcare, multi-party problems |
| Drivers tree | One metric to move (wait time, ridership, emissions) |

Then the player writes 3 buckets as questions from a free word bank. The aircraft's cabin lights come on one row at a time for each good bucket; a recited, generic bucket leaves the row dark.

**AIRCRAFT** *(after the first good bucket)*: There. That one fits.

**AIRCRAFT** *(after the third)*: You didn't bring me a framework. You built one for this question. That's what they'll be listening for at nine.

**YOU:** Will I remember any of this?

**AIRCRAFT:** You'll remember the first sentence. Start every case with it.

**Ending.** *The tug pushes the aircraft back as the sun comes up. You wake at your desk at 6 a.m. Page 38 is open. You turn to a blank page, write one line, the objective, and smile.*

**Rank screen.** Shows the five gauges across all cases played, the weakest gauge, and a suggested replay for it (for example, low Communication suggests Reyes's cases, where answer-first closes refill his patience meter).

## Implementation notes

Every case shares one data shape, so the script above can be loaded as content files and the engine only needs one case loop.

**Case file schema** (one file per case):

```yaml
id: tank_farm
case_type: profitability
host: { name: Okafor, portrait: okafor, trait: heard_every_question }
item: fuel_chit
variants:
  industry: [regional_airline, grocery_chain]
  twist: [client_problem, market_problem]
  data: [revenue_first, market_first]
beats:
  objective:
    tiles: { goal: [...], metric: [...], timeframe: [...] }
    correct: { goal: 0, metric: 0, timeframe: 0 }
  lanterns:
    max: 3
    questions:
      - text: "Are competitors seeing the same decline?"
        useful: true
        answer: { regional_airline: "...", grocery_chain: "..." }
  board:
    buckets:
      - text: "Is it the market or the client?"
        status: critical
        subpoints_correct: { regional_airline: [...], grocery_chain: [...] }
        subpoints_decoy: ["Fuel, obviously"]
      - text: "Market"
        status: decoy_label
        coach_line: "That's a word. I need a question."
  flag:
    best_start: market_or_client
    hypotheses: { client_problem: 0, market_problem: 1 }
  data_cards: [...]
  math_cards: [...]
  close:
    slots: [recommendation, reason_1, reason_2, risk, next_step]
  partner_question: from_lowest_ranked_bucket
hints: ["nudge", "pointer", "near_answer"]
clickpoints:
  - object: fuel_gauge
    lines: ["...", "...", "..."]
    after_last: repeat_last
```

**State flags**

| Flag | Set when | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `variant_seed` | Case entered | Picks industry, twist, data options (8 combos) |
| `bucket_rank[]` | Flag planted and buckets ordered | Drawing the Partner's Question from the lowest-ranked bucket |
| `hints_used[beat]` | Thought bubble clicked | Half-star deduction per level |
| `coached[beat]` | Decoy picked, then fixed | Presence star if fixed on the next try |
| `seen_variants[case]` | Case closed | Replay prefers unseen combos first |
| `case_cleared[]` | Close completed | Three items unlock the Remote Stand; all 8 count toward rank |
| `click_count[object]` | Clickpoint clicked | Escalating line index |

**Variant selection rule.** On first visit, use Option A for all three slots so the first play is the cleanest version. On replay, draw randomly from combos not yet seen.

**Clickpoint library rule.** Every object gets 2 to 4 lines. Line 1 is plain, line 2 is the same joke escalated, the last line either breaks the fourth wall or slips in a real case tip. After the last line, repeat it.

**Text-box notes.** All dialogue is written for text boxes with no voice, short enough to fit two lines. Stage directions in italics are animation cues (a character action or a mouth-frame change), not displayed text. Speaker tags map straight onto your existing portrait files: moss-1/2/3, okafor-1/2/3, reyes-1/2/3, dana-1/2/3 and plane-1/2/3 (closed, half open, wide open).

**Content to add next**, in order of interview frequency: new product launch, competitive response, and make vs buy, each using the same schema.
